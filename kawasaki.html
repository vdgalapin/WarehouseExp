Overview

At Kawasaki, I work as a software engineer focused on warehouse management systems. My role covers the full lifecycle of operational software: researching warehouse processes, speaking with users, designing workflows, building the applications, documenting them, and supporting them after deployment.

I developed systems used across receiving, warehouse operations, quality control, planning, production support, and shipping-related processes. Most of these tools were designed for mobile use to support real warehouse movement, while web and desktop screens provided visibility for departments that needed to monitor, review, and manage activity.

The result is a connected set of tools that improves inventory accuracy, part traceability, operational control, and communication between warehouse, QC, planning, production, and linefeed teams.

Detailed Responsibilities and Systems Built

1. Receiving Systems

I built receiving workflows that allow materials to be received by box, pallet, container, or ASN. These solutions were used in both mobile and Windows-based applications and supported barcode and QR code scanning for fast identification.

The purpose of the receiving system was not only to record inbound shipments, but also to create reliable visibility from the moment parts entered the plant. I made sure the workflows handled receipts from individual suppliers, multiple suppliers, and transfers from other plants.

This improved traceability and gave QC, Planning, Warehouse, and Production a dependable source of inbound information. It also helped control supplier data quality by making sure shipments were recorded correctly and exceptions could be identified early.

2. Warehouse Putaway and Location Management

I developed warehouse functions that allowed received items to be moved into the correct storage areas based on operational needs. The system supported different handling rules for large items, small items, bulk storage, QC locations, and production-facing areas.

Because not every item can be managed the same way, I built logic that supports standard locations, bulk areas, and status-based handling. This gave warehouse teams a structured way to store and control materials, even when packaging, size, or identification requirements were different.

Most warehouse transactions were supported through mobile applications for operational mobility, while web-based screens provided the broader visibility needed by QC, Planning, Production, and Linefeed teams.

3. QC Control System

I also built QC-focused system features that allow the quality team to stop questionable material from being used by Production or Linefeed. If an item is under review, scrap, or otherwise in question, QC can take control of it in the system and direct the next action.

This includes moving items for inspection, scrapping them, or returning them to warehouse or assembly when they are approved. This tool is important because quality decisions directly affect material availability, planning decisions, and accounting exposure tied to waste, demand, and inventory value.

4. Cycle Count and Inventory Accuracy

I built cycle count functionality that allows the team to correct inventory by location and maintain warehouse accuracy systematically. This made inventory adjustments more controlled and gave the business a more reliable picture of what is physically in the warehouse.

That visibility matters beyond warehouse operations. Planning needs accurate stock information to understand replenishment needs, and Accounting depends on controlled inventory records to understand material value and financial impact.

5. Requesting, Picking, Carting, Delivery, and Returns

I developed a connected warehouse execution flow that manages how parts move through the warehouse and into production. This included requesting, picking, carting, delivery, and returns to warehouse.

The requesting process used the ARS (Automated Request System), which systematically calculates material requests based on production demand and sends those needs to Linefeed and warehouse operations.

The picking process then uses those requests to determine what to pick and where to pick it from. Depending on the item, the system can choose FIFO-driven locations, bulk storage, or a specific location inside a bulk area for partial picks. I also built logic that helps calculate routes more efficiently to support warehouse productivity.

After picking, materials are moved into carting so they can be tracked before delivery. This improves control and accuracy before the final handoff. I also developed the reverse process for returns to warehouse so unused material could move back through a controlled workflow instead of being handled informally.

6. Inventory Consumption and Shipping Support

As items are consumed on the production line, the system automatically subtracts them from inventory. This keeps warehouse records aligned with actual usage and supports downstream production reporting.

I also worked on the finished goods side through a related system that supports shipping preparation, including BOM creation and required shipping paperwork. This helped connect warehouse execution with output readiness.

7. Ownership, Support, and Documentation

My role was not limited to development. I also supported the system in operation by fixing bugs, resolving process and system issues, documenting functionality, and helping both production and programming teams keep the applications stable and usable.

That means I contributed across research, communication, design, development, support, and documentation — not just writing code, but making sure the system worked in real warehouse conditions and continued to support the plant after release.

6. WMS Administration, Labels, Printers, and User Support

In addition to developing process workflows, I managed important operational controls around the WMS itself. I built and administered the user management structure for the system, including roles, levels, permissions, and controlled access across the different applications used by warehouse, QC, planning, production, and linefeed teams.

I also designed labels for boxes, shipments, crates, pallets, and other handling scenarios so that the physical warehouse process stayed aligned with the digital transaction flow. That included working with printers, print destinations, and setup validation to make sure labels were produced correctly and at the right point in the operation.

Alongside system administration, I supported licensing, user handling, issue resolution, and operational support to keep the platform stable and usable. This made my role both technical and operational: I was not only building the system, but also managing how people used it in the real warehouse environment.
